Learn Yoga Course Design with Phoebe · Session 3 of 6

Progressions across weeks

A good class has a peak. A good programme has a direction: each pose grows out of one taught earlier, and each week asks a little more than the last without asking everything at once. Tonight we name the three levers a teacher can move, look honestly at the training-science idea of planned waves and why researchers still argue about it, and borrow one rule from motor learning about when to repeat a pose and when to mix it in. Then you write three progressions for Juniper that session 4's bench will check.

Part 0

Where we start

The running case is constructed. Juniper Studio is an invented community studio; its owner Mei has commissioned an 8-week beginner programme, one 60-minute class a week, for twelve students. Session 1 gave you the charter and the four phases. Session 2 gave you the intake and the cohort's caution flags: wrist 3, knee 2, balance 1, back 1, none 5. Tonight decides what changes from week to week, and by how much.

Not medical advice. The holds and ranges on this page are constructed teaching values for healthy beginners, not a dose for any condition. Every movement shown carries a "Stop if" line. A student whose pain does not improve after a few weeks, is worse at night, or comes with unexplained weight loss should see a GP; numbness or weakness in both legs, or bladder or bowel changes, means 999 or A&E (NHS back pain page). Part 1 · covers ACSM 2009, Garber 2011, Thomas 2018 vs Konrad 2024

Three levers, moved one at a time

5 min live

Every step up in a yoga progression moves at least one of three levers. Load: how much body weight and effort the pose asks for. Complexity: how many things the student has to coordinate at once, balance included. Hold time or repetitions: how long, or how many times. Move one per step and you can see which one a student is not ready for. Move all three and you cannot.

LiveWhat the exercise guidelines say, and what they do not3 min▶

The American College of Sports Medicine's 2009 position stand on resistance training recommends that "a 2-10% increase in load be applied when the individual can perform the current workload for one to two repetitions over the desired number", and asks that progression be applied in context. That is a clean rule: progress when the current step is managed with something to spare, and progress by a small amount.

Say plainly what it is about. The 2 to 10 percent figure is for external load, weights on a bar or a machine. A yoga pose has no dial marked in percent, and nobody has tested the rule on poses. Using it in a yoga room is an inference: keep the logic (move on when the current step is comfortable with reserve, move by a small step) and drop the number.

The 2011 ACSM guidance (Garber) adds two pieces a yoga teacher can use more directly: flexibility exercise "a total of 60 s per exercise" on two or more days a week, and the line that "gradual progression of exercise intensity and volume may reduce the risks". Note the "may". It is a reasonable precaution, not a proven safety effect.

Self-studyDoes weekly stretching time matter? Two reviews disagree3 min▶

Thomas and colleagues (2018) reviewed stretching programmes and concluded that weekly stretching time matters, with gains associated with 5 minutes or more a week. Konrad and colleagues (2024, J Sport Health Sci) pooled 77 studies and found that volume, intensity and frequency made no significant difference to range-of-motion gains. Both are systematic reviews; they asked slightly different questions of overlapping literatures and reached different answers. Nobody has settled it.

One finding is more consistent: a second Konrad review (2024, Sports Med) found that static stretching range-of-motion gains were already significant in programmes of 4 weeks or less. For an 8-week beginner programme that is encouraging. Students can notice change inside your programme, so you do not need to push holds hard to get there.

What a teacher can do with an unresolved question: choose a sensible hold (Garber's 60 seconds per exercise across the week is a fair anchor), keep it steady, and treat hold time as the gentlest lever, the one to move first before load or complexity.

Self-studyWhere the lever ideas come from in a single class1 min▶

How a single pose is built, which joints load and how to cue it, belongs to Yoga Theory session 6: asana theory ↗ and session 5: functional anatomy ↗. Here we only ask which week each version of a pose belongs in.

Real world

The jump from tabletop to crow. A familiar pattern: a teacher, pleased with a group's progress, goes from tabletop and plank to an arm balance in one week. Load, complexity and the head-forward position all change at once. When half the room cannot do it, there is no way to tell which change was the problem, and the wrist-flagged students have no rung to step back to. One lever per week keeps the diagnosis simple and the ladder intact.

Part 2 · covers Williams 2017, Afonso 2019, Kiely 2012/2018, Anyadike-Danes 2023

Periodisation: useful idea, disputed evidence

5 min live

Periodisation is the training-science idea of planning load in waves: build, ease off, build again. Juniper's plan uses it, with lighter weeks 4 and 7. The idea is widely used and genuinely argued about, and a teacher should know both halves before promising a studio owner it works.

LiveThe case for, the case against3 min▶

For. Williams and colleagues (2017) pooled 18 studies with 81 effects and found periodised programmes beat non-periodised ones for maximal strength, effect size 0.43 (95% CI 0.27 to 0.58), with undulating models (load that goes up and down) looking more favourable.

Against, or at least not so fast. Afonso and colleagues (2019) looked closely at what those comparisons were: "None of the 21 studies included in the two meta-analyses compared periodized programs with varied, non-periodized programs." The non-periodised groups mostly did the same thing every week. So the evidence shows that planned variation beats no variation. It does not show that a planned wave beats sensible, unplanned variation.

A third view. Kiely (2012) is not rejecting periodisation. His argument is that training responses vary widely between people, so a fixed plan written weeks ahead should give way to a responsive one that adjusts to how people are actually doing. Kiely (2018) goes further and argues that the stress model periodisation was built on needs re-evaluating.

Those three positions are all current. Nobody has settled it, and you should not either.

Self-studyWhat coaches actually believe2 min▶

Anyadike-Danes and colleagues (2023) surveyed 106 coaches. 71 percent called their approach periodisation. Only 33 percent agreed that adaptations are achievable in fixed timeframes, and only 10 percent supported fixed targets. In other words, most practitioners use the word and few trust a rigid calendar. That is close to the practical reading below.

All of this research is on athletes and strength outcomes, not on yoga beginners. Carrying it into a yoga room is a borrowing, and a reasonable one only at the level of the principle.

Self-studyThe practical reading for a yoga programme2 min▶

Plan the waves, then adjust to the room. Juniper's lighter weeks 4 and 7 are written in for two reasons that do not depend on winning the periodisation argument: they give absent students a week to catch up without falling further behind (session 1's attendance numbers), and they give everyone a chance to own what they have learned. If the room arrives in week 5 tired or stiff, the plan bends; if they arrive keen, it can step up a rung. A plan is the default, not a contract.

For the wider idea of shaping a learning arc across a course, see Course Design: the arc ↗.

Real world

The "harder every week" promise. Many studio course pages promise each week will be more challenging than the last. It sells. It also commits the teacher to the linear line on the figure, with no week to consolidate and no room to respond when a cohort is struggling. "Each week builds on the last, with lighter weeks to let it settle" is just as honest a promise and leaves the teacher free to teach the room in front of them.

Part 3 · covers Shea and Morgan 1979, Brady 2004 and 2008, Guadagnoli and Lee 2004

Block it first, then mix it

5 min live

Once a pose is in the programme, when should it come back, and in what company? Motor-learning research on how practice is ordered gives a useful answer, with real caveats about how far it travels outside the laboratory.

LiveMixed practice is remembered better, with caveats3 min▶

In 1979 Shea and Morgan had people learn several movement patterns either in blocks (all of one, then all of the next) or in a random order. The random-order group retained and transferred the skills better. Reviews report the other half of the finding: during practice itself, the blocked group looked better. That gap, worse-looking practice but better learning, is called the contextual interference effect.

How big is it in real life? Brady's 2004 meta-analysis of 61 studies (139 effects) put the overall effect at 0.38, but 0.57 in basic laboratory research against 0.19 in applied settings, and 0.50 for adults against 0.10 for younger learners. His 2008 update called the effect "considerably weaker in applied settings". So it is real, smaller outside the lab, and stronger for adults like Juniper's students.

Guadagnoli and Lee's challenge point framework (2004) explains why it is not universal: the right amount of difficulty depends on the performer's skill. Mixing helps a learner who already has the basic pattern. For a true beginner, the same mixing can simply be too much.

Self-studyThe block-then-mix rule, labelled as what it is2 min▶

Put Brady's adult finding together with the challenge point and you get a working rule: teach a new pose in a block (two neighbouring weeks, the same place in the class, the same lead-in) until students have the basic shape, then mix it into longer and different sequences. That rule is an inference from motor-learning research on other skills. No study has tested it on yoga poses across weeks. Say so if anyone asks.

What about how many days apart practice should be? A study by Shea and colleagues (2000) addressed spacing across days, but we have not been able to verify its findings, so no number is quoted here. The best-studied spacing research is on verbal learning, and it lives in Reading: spacing and forgetting ↗; it is not re-taught here because word lists are not poses.

Real world

The class that looked great and remembered nothing. A common pattern in drop-in studios: the same flow every week, perfectly smooth by week 4, and students who freeze the moment a guest teacher changes the order. Blocked practice looked like learning. Mixing known poses into new orders in the later weeks is what lets students take their practice into an open class, which was Mei's brief in the first place.

Build-along

Three progressions for Juniper

★ 22 min · everyone builds

A progression is a short chain of poses where each one is the prerequisite for the next and each step moves one lever. Here are three families from Juniper's wave plan (constructed), laid on the weeks they first appear. Then you write the chains, regressions and stop-if lines yourself, and build a fourth family of your own.

Pick the family. Choose a group of poses that share a base position: weight on the hands, standing on the legs, lifting the chest from the floor. Juniper's three are arm support, standing and back bend. Check the cohort flags first: arm support touches the 3 wrist-flagged students, so its regressions matter most.

★ Step 1 · Juniper's families (constructed)ARM SUPPORT wrist flag x3 in the room STANDING knee flag x2 (low lunge), balance x1 (Warrior III) BACK BEND back flag x1 (low cobra, locust)

Write the chain with prerequisites. Work backwards from the hardest pose you want, naming what each one needs first. Every prerequisite must appear in an earlier week.

★ Step 2 · chainsTabletop -> Downward dog -> Plank (Tabletop is the prerequisite for both dog and plank) Low lunge -> High lunge -> Warrior I -> Warrior III (Warrior III also needs Tree) Sphinx -> Low cobra; Sphinx -> Locust

Choose one lever per step. Load, complexity or hold time. If a step seems to move two, split it into two steps or two weeks. Where the next pose sits at the same constructed level, the change is the shape itself: hold it for fewer breaths at first, then build the breaths.

★ Step 3 · one lever eachTabletop -> Downward dog LOAD (more weight on the hands) Tabletop -> Plank LOAD (knees lift, more weight forward) Low lunge -> High lunge LOAD (back knee lifts) High lunge -> Warrior I SAME LEVEL, NEW SHAPE (hold briefly first) Warrior I -> Warrior III COMPLEXITY (balance on one leg) Sphinx -> Low cobra / Locust LOAD

Set the regression and the "stop if" for each. The regression is the rung back down, offered to everyone, never singled out. The "stop if" is the signal to come out and rest, and if it keeps happening, to talk to a clinician. You are adapting, not assessing.

★ Step 4 · regression and stop ifTabletop forearms on blocks, pad under the knees stop if: wrist or knee pain Downward dog hands on a wall or a chair seat stop if: wrist pain, dizziness Plank plank against a wall stop if: wrist pain, low back sags and aches Low lunge pad under the back knee, or a short standing lunge stop if: knee pain High lunge back heel down, one hand on a wall stop if: knee pain, feeling unsteady Warrior III hands on a wall, standing leg soft stop if: losing balance, knee pain Low cobra sphinx, small range stop if: pinching or pain in the low back Locust one leg at a time, chest down stop if: pinching or pain in the low back

Place on the weeks with the block-then-mix rule. Each new pose: two neighbouring weeks in the same spot, then back inside different sequences later. Nothing new in the lighter weeks 4 and 7. Check against the wave plan: Plank weeks 3, 5, 6, 8; High lunge and Warrior I weeks 5, 6, 8; Warrior III weeks 6 and 8; Locust weeks 5, 6, 8.

★ Step 5 · weeks (wave plan)wk 1 Tabletop, Sphinx (foundation) wk 2 Downward dog, Low cobra (block starts) wk 3 Plank, Low lunge (block starts) wk 4 lighter: nothing new wk 5 High lunge, Warrior I, Locust wk 6 Warrior III; the wk 5 poses again (block) wk 7 lighter: nothing new wk 8 everything mixed into longer sequences

Now your own family. Pick a fourth family for Juniper or for the programme you chartered in session 1: seated folds, balance, twists. Write the chain, one lever per step, the regression and "stop if" for each, and the weeks. Session 4's bench checks all four families for prerequisite gaps, spikes and one-off poses.

★ Step 6 · your familyFAMILY: ______________ FLAGS IT TOUCHES: ______ CHAIN: ______ -> ______ -> ______ -> ______ LEVER PER STEP: ______ / ______ / ______ REGRESSION + STOP IF: one line per pose WEEKS: block in wk __ and __, mixed in wk __

Hold time first, shape second. When a student is ready for more, the cheapest step is a breath or two longer in the pose they already own. Changing the shape (load or complexity) is the bigger step and earns its own week.

20 min

Three questions

Clear all three and session 3 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · The ACSM 2009 position stand recommends a 2-10% increase in load once the current workload is managed with one to two repetitions to spare. How should a yoga teacher use it?

The figure is for external load in resistance training. Its transfer to yoga is an inference, so the principle travels and the percentage does not.

2 · What is the most accurate summary of the periodisation evidence?

Williams 2017 found the 0.43 effect; Afonso 2019 pointed out the comparison was mostly against constant training. Kiely argues for responsive systems, not rejection, and only 10 percent of surveyed coaches supported fixed targets.

3 · Brady's 2004 meta-analysis of contextual interference found an overall effect of 0.38. What else did it find?

Mixed practice helps, less outside the lab and more for adults. Block-then-mix for yoga is an inference from this and the challenge point framework, not a tested yoga finding.
