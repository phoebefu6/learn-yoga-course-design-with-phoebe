One room, many levels

Every beginner room is a mixed room. Twelve people book the same programme and arrive with stiff wrists, a sore knee, a wobble on one leg, and one person who joins in week 5 with a note from their doctor. Tonight we look at what research on mixed groups actually shows, build options ladders and props into the plan before anyone walks in, learn to offer a choice without turning it into a ranking, and plan Juniper's week 5 so every flagged pose already has its options card.

Where we start

The running case is constructed. Juniper Studio is an invented community studio; its owner Mei has twelve students booked into an 8-week beginner programme, one 60-minute class a week. Session 2's intake gave us the cohort's caution flags: wrist 3, knee 2, balance 1, back 1, none 5. Session 4's bench then broke the plan on purpose: a student with a head-below-heart flag joins in week 5. Tonight that room becomes the design brief. Bring a chair, a strap and two blocks if you have them; you will want to try the ladders yourself.

Not medical advice. A caution flag is a referral tag, never a diagnosis. The teacher adapts the pose and, when something is beyond a teacher's scope, refers the student to their clinician. Nothing here assesses a condition or promises that an option will fix one.

Part 1 · covers Deunk 2018 and Smale-Jacobse 2019

What the research on mixed groups shows

5 min live

There is almost no research on mixed-level yoga rooms. There is a body of research on mixed-ability school classrooms, where the question is the same shape: do you sort people into levels, or teach one group with options inside it? The two best reviews are honest about what they cannot say, and we should be too.

LiveTwo reviews, one caution3 min▶

Marjolein Deunk and colleagues (2018) pooled 21 studies and 78 effects on differentiation in primary school maths and language. Differentiation built into broader programmes showed "small to moderate positive effects". Homogeneous ability grouping, sorting children into same-level groups, had a "small negative effect on low-ability students, but no effect on others." Their summary is worth pinning up: grouping alone "is not enough to guarantee differentiated instruction."

Annemieke Smale-Jacobse and colleagues (2019) looked at secondary schools and found only 14 papers on 12 studies that qualified. Summarised effects ranged from d = 0.741 to 0.509 once an outlier was left out, and the authors were blunt: "there are still severe knowledge gaps." So the evidence points one way, it is thin, and it is about classrooms, not movement.

Self-studyWhat transfers to a yoga room, and what does not3 min▶

Both reviews measured school academic outcomes: test scores in maths and reading. A yoga programme cares about something different, whether a student can do a pose safely, feels capable, and keeps coming. Carrying the finding across is an inference, not a result, and it is fair to say so to a studio owner who asks.

The practical reading is still useful. If sorting learners into a fixed lower group carried a cost for exactly the people who most needed support, a "level 1 / level 2" split inside a beginner programme deserves suspicion. The alternative the reviews favour, options inside one shared plan, is also the one that keeps a beginner programme a single group that can travel through eight weeks together. For the general craft of adapting a course to its learners, see Course Design: needs analysis ↗.

Real world

The "beginners' corner". A familiar studio pattern: the teacher asks newer students to set up at the back, near the wall, "so you can take it easy". It is kindly meant. It also sorts the room by perceived level before anyone has moved, and the people at the back learn they are the lower group. Putting the wall option within reach of every mat, and props beside everyone, does the same practical job without the label.

Part 2 · covers CAST UDL Guidelines 3.0 and Saper 2017 props

Options ladders, built in from the start

5 min live

An option you invent on the spot for one student is a rescue. An option you designed before week 1, with the props already by the mats, is part of the programme. Design frameworks and the best-documented yoga trial agree on building it in.

LiveOptions as a default, not a rescue3 min▶

CAST's Universal Design for Learning Guidelines, version 3.0 (released 30 July 2024), organise design around three principles, Engagement, Representation, and Action and Expression, broken into nine guidelines and 36 considerations. The aim is that "all learners are able to access and participate in meaningful, challenging learning opportunities." UDL is a design framework, not an outcome trial: it tells you how to plan, not how much better a class will go.

Saper's 2017 trial is the yoga version of the same idea, in practice. Its 12 weekly classes were taught to a predominantly low-income, racially diverse group where "pose variations and aids (such as chair, strap, and blocks) accommodated various abilities," with fewer than 5 participants per instructor. The variations were part of the manual, not improvised. That is the move: write the ladder into the plan for every pose that carries a flag.

Self-studyWhy the wall rung exists3 min▶

Jasien and colleagues (2015) measured eye pressure in 10 people with glaucoma and 10 without during 2-minute holds. In downward dog, pressure in the glaucoma group rose from 17 to 28 mmHg (17 to 29 in the normal group), and returned to baseline within 2 minutes of sitting up. Whether that harms anyone's eyes over time was not tested. A teacher does not need to interpret any of that; it is the reason a "head below heart" flag exists, and the reason the wall rung keeps the head up.

A good ladder rung keeps the pose's job. Downward dog lengthens the back of the body with the arms overhead; the wall and chair rungs do that too, with less weight on the hands and the head higher. A rung that changes the job, swapping in an unrelated pose, belongs in a different conversation. How each pose works is Yoga Theory session 6 ↗; here we only decide which rungs the programme offers.

Real world

The props cupboard at the back. Many studios keep blocks and straps in a cupboard, and students fetch them only when told. In a mixed room that means the student who needs a block has to stand up, cross the room and admit it. Teachers who put two blocks and a strap beside every mat before class find more people use them, and nobody's choice is visible.

Part 3 · covers offering choice without ranking, and the late joiner

Choice without a ranking

5 min live

The ladder is half the design. The other half is the sentence you say while everyone is on their mat. The same three options can be offered as a menu or as a ranking, and students hear the difference at once. What follows is practitioner convention, widely shared and sensible, with no trial behind it.

LiveFour wording habits3 min▶

Name options by what they do, not by level. "Hands on the wall keeps your head up; hands on the floor is the full shape" rather than "the easy version". Offer the regression first, or as an equal. If the full pose always comes first, everything after it sounds like a step down. Never single out a flagged student. Offer the menu to the whole room; the student with the flag already knows which card is theirs from the intake conversation. Show every option yourself at some point in the programme, so no option belongs to "those people".

How to phrase a cue well in general is Yoga Theory session 8 ↗. The programme-level job is narrower: write the neutral wording for each options card before class, so it is not improvised under pressure.

Self-studyThe late joiner and the ratio problem4 min▶

Session 4's bench sent a student with a head-below-heart flag into week 5. On the wave plan (rung 4), 3 poses then needed a regression: downward dog in weeks 5, 6 and 8. On the linear plan (rung 3), 9 did: downward dog, dolphin, crow and supported headstand across weeks 5 to 8. The ladder you drew in Part 2 is the whole answer for rung 4: the wall rung, offered to the room as an equal choice, and the new student is never the only one doing something different.

Ratio is the honest limit. Saper's trial ran under 5 participants per instructor. A studio class of 12 with one teacher, Juniper's set-up, is more than twice that, so the teacher cannot coach every rung individually. The constructed bench numbers will be different for your plan, and the point holds: the more options a plan needs, the more they must be designed in, set up and worded in advance, because there will be no spare attention to invent them mid-class. If a student reports dizziness or pressure that does not settle, they stop, sit, and are referred to their clinician; session 6 lists the NHS warning signs.

Real world

"If you can't do it, take the easy version." Most teachers have heard this line, and many have said it. It packs a ranking and a failure into one sentence. The fix costs nothing: "Two ways in. Hands on the wall keeps your head up, hands on the floor is the full shape. Pick the one that suits you today." Same poses, same timing, and the person at the wall is simply doing the pose.

Build-along

Week 5 for a mixed room

★ 22 min · everyone builds

Juniper's week 5 (constructed) opens the integrate phase: the heaviest pose list so far, and the week the late joiner arrives. You will take the 14 poses from the wave plan, mark which carry a flag for this cohort, write a three-option card for each, word it neutrally, and plan where the props go before anyone arrives.

List week 5 in order. Copy the pose list straight from the plan the bench tested. Do not add or swap anything yet; the options go on top of the plan, not instead of it.

★ Step 1 · Juniper week 5 (constructed)Chair, Tabletop, Low lunge, High lunge, Warrior I, Triangle, Tree, Downward dog, Plank, Low cobra, Locust, Pigeon, Seated twist, Rest 14 poses · integrate phase · late joiner arrives

Mark the flagged poses for this cohort. Cross each pose against the intake flags, then add the late joiner's flag. A pose can carry more than one.

★ Step 2 · flags in the roomwrist (3 students) -> Tabletop, Downward dog, Plank knee (2) -> Chair, Tabletop, Low lunge, Pigeon balance (1) -> High lunge, Tree back (1) -> Low cobra, Locust head below heart (1, late joiner) -> Downward dog No flag: Warrior I, Triangle, Seated twist, Rest

Write a three-option card per flagged pose. Full pose, one regression that keeps the pose's job, and rest. Take the regression from the pose library so it matches what the bench assumed.

★ Step 3 · options cards (excerpt)DOWNWARD DOG 1 floor 2 hands on a wall or chair seat 3 rest TABLETOP 1 full 2 forearms on blocks, pad under knees 3 rest TREE 1 full 2 toes on the floor, hand on a wall 3 rest LOCUST 1 full 2 one leg at a time, chest down 3 rest PIGEON 1 full 2 lying figure four 3 rest

Write the neutral wording for each card. One sentence per pose that names what each option does. Read it aloud: if any option sounds like a step down, rewrite it.

★ Step 4 · neutral wordingDOG: "Hands on the wall keeps your head up. Hands on the chair seat is lighter on the wrists. Hands on the floor is the full shape. Pick one for today." TREE: "Toes on the floor and a hand on the wall gives you a steady base. Foot higher is more balance work." LOCUST: "One leg at a time, or both. Chest stays down."

Plan prop placement before class. Decide where every prop and every wall space is, so no student has to fetch anything or move across the room to take an option.

★ Step 5 · set-upEvery mat: 2 blocks, 1 strap, 1 folded blanket (knee pad) Mats along the walls: wall rung for dog, tree, high lunge 2 chairs at the side for the chair rung of dog Late joiner: mat near a wall, like several others, so the wall rung is in reach without being singled out

Now do your own week. Take the busiest week of your own plan from session 4. Mark its flags, write the cards, word them, and plan the set-up. Keep it; session 6 turns the last week into a handover.

Ten of fourteen is normal. In a twelve-person beginner room, most poses will carry a flag for somebody. That is not a sign the plan is wrong. It is the reason the options belong in the plan, written and set up before week 1, rather than invented for one student while eleven wait.

Homework

Before session 6

20 min
  1. Finish every card. Write the three-option card and neutral wording for all ten flagged poses in Juniper's week 5, or for every flagged pose in your own week.
  2. Try the downward dog ladder yourself. Wall, chair, floor, using the holds and stop-if lines in the guide. Notice what each rung asks of your wrists and where your head ends up.
  3. Listen for ranking words. In the next class you take or teach, count "easy", "advanced", "if you can't" and "level". Rewrite each as what the option does.
  4. Check your ratio. Write down how many students per teacher your own programme will run with, and mark which options will need set-up before class because there will be no time during it.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 5 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · What did Deunk and colleagues (2018) find about homogeneous ability grouping in primary schools?

Sorting into same-level groups showed a small negative effect for low-ability students and none for others, while differentiation within broader programmes was small to moderate positive. These are school outcomes; carrying them into a yoga room is an inference.

2 · Why does the downward dog options ladder include a hands-on-the-wall rung?

The wall rung keeps the pose's job with the head up and less weight on the hands, which is why it serves the head-below-heart and wrist flags. The teacher offers it; the teacher never assesses a condition, and no option promises to fix one.

3 · Which cue follows the convention of choice without a ranking?

Name each option by what it does, offer the regression first or as an equal, and never single out a flagged student. It is practitioner convention rather than a tested finding, and it costs nothing to do.
