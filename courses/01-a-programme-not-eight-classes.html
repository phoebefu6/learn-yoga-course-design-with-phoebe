Learn Yoga Course Design with Phoebe · Session 1 of 6

A programme is not eight classes in a row

Most new teachers can plan a good class. A programme asks a different question: what should be possible in week 8 that was not possible in week 1, and what does each week have to make possible for the next? Tonight we look at what three published 12-week yoga programmes actually ran, count how many classes their students really came to, draw the four phases of a beginner arc, and write the one-page charter that every later session builds on.

Part 0

Where we start

The running case is constructed. Juniper Studio is an invented community studio in an invented town. Its owner, Mei, has asked you for an 8-week beginner programme: one 60-minute class a week, twelve places, people who have never done yoga or tried it once on a video. Her brief is one line long: "By the end they should feel confident coming to an open class." Everything you build for Juniper across the six sessions is something you can build for a real studio.

Not medical advice. These pages are about planning a programme. They do not teach anyone to diagnose a condition, treat it, or clear a student for exercise. A student with chest pain, dizziness, a recent injury, pain that gets worse with movement, or a condition their doctor manages should talk to that clinician first. Session 2 builds an intake that routes them there, and session 6 lists the warning signs.

Part 1 · covers Sherman 2011, Tilbrook 2011, Saper 2017

What published programmes actually ran

5 min live

The best-documented multi-week yoga programmes are in clinical trials for chronic low back pain. We are not using them for their pain results, which belong to clinicians. We are using them because the trial teams had to write the programme down: how many weeks, how long each class, what changed from week to week, and what students did at home.

LiveRepetition was built in, on purpose3 min▶

Karen Sherman's team ran 12 weekly 75-minute classes for 228 adults. Their write-up is unusually specific: "Six distinct and progressive classes were taught in pairs." Week 1 and week 2 used the same class plan, weeks 3 and 4 the next, and so on. The whole programme used 17 relatively simple postures, five to eleven in any one class, and asked students to practise 20 minutes on days without a class.

Helen Tilbrook's UK trial describes "a 12-class, gradually progressing yoga program delivered by 12 teachers over 3 months." Robert Saper's trial ran 12 weekly 75-minute classes where "pose variations and aids (such as chair, strap, and blocks) accommodated various abilities," and strongly encouraged 30 minutes of home practice a day. Three teams, three countries' worth of students, the same shape: a short list of poses, repeated, made a little harder over time, with practice in between.

Self-studyWhat these trials can and cannot tell a teacher3 min▶

They tell you how carefully designed programmes were built and how real people used them. They do not tell you that yoga treats back pain in your room. Sherman's yoga group did better than people given a self-care book, and no better than a stretching class at any point; Saper's yoga was found noninferior to physical therapy. Those comparisons are clinical questions, and making health claims about a programme is outside a teacher's scope (session 2).

They also hide the hardest design detail. A 2021 review of 22 tai chi, qigong and yoga programmes for osteoarthritis checked each against a reporting checklist: only one (5 percent) reported a progression rule, and only one reported a starting level rule. Most published programmes do not say how they decided what to make harder, or when. That is the gap these six sessions fill.

Real world

The teacher who planned twelve different classes. A common first programme: twelve beautifully varied classes, a new theme and a new peak pose every week. It feels generous. On the trials' evidence it is the opposite of how published programmes were built, and session 4's bench will show what it does to prerequisites. Variety is not the enemy; variety without repetition is.

Part 2 · covers attendance and home practice in the same trials

Plan for seven of twelve

5 min live

A programme plan assumes people turn up. The trials counted. These were motivated volunteers with free classes and study staff checking in, so real studios will rarely do better.

LiveDesign for the student who misses week 33 min▶

In Sherman's trial 65 percent of the yoga group came to at least 8 of 12 classes. In Tilbrook's, 60 percent met a bar of at least 3 of the first 6 sessions and 3 more. In Saper's, with a predominantly low-income and racially diverse sample, the median was 7 classes, and 44 percent reached the 9 classes the team had set as adherent. A 2016 review of 168 yoga trials put average dropout at about 11 percent, and dropping out is not the same as missing a class: people who stay enrolled still miss weeks.

So a plan where week 5 only works if you were there in week 4 will fail for a large share of the room. The fix is the one the trials used: teach each new element at least twice, in neighbouring weeks, so one absence does not break the chain. The bench in session 4 counts "one-off" poses, taught in a single week, for exactly this reason.

Self-studyHome practice fades, so do not hang the plan on it2 min▶

Sherman's yoga students practised a median of 100 minutes a week at home by week 6, and 60 by week 12. Among Saper's yoga students who reported home practice (75 percent of them), the median was 27 minutes on 4 days a week, against the 30 minutes a day the team had encouraged. Home practice is worth asking for; it is not something to build the progression on. Session 6 comes back to keeping practice alive after the last class.

A sentence for the studio owner. "I plan as if most students will come to about two classes in three, so every new pose is taught twice and nobody falls behind for missing a week." Owners hear that as care, not pessimism.

Part 3 · covers the four-phase arc

Foundation, build, integrate, consolidate

5 min live

The trials tell us programmes repeated and progressed gradually; they rarely say how. A practical frame many teachers use splits a programme into four phases. It is a planning convention, not a research finding, and the names matter less than what each phase is for. Here it is drawn on the 8-week plan Juniper will test on the bench.

LiveWhat each phase is for3 min▶

Foundation (weeks 1 and 2) teaches the positions everything else stands on: standing, tabletop, the first back bend, how to rest. Build (weeks 3 and 4) adds one new demand at a time on top of those, then a lighter week to let it settle. Integrate (weeks 5 and 6) links known poses into longer sequences and adds the hardest poses the programme will reach. Consolidate (weeks 7 and 8) repeats and refines, with one lighter week, so students leave able to do what they learned without you.

The numbers on the figure are the bench's: each pose has a constructed load level from 1 to 5, and a week's load is the sum. The ACSM's exercise guidance says "gradual progression of exercise intensity and volume may reduce the risks of exercise" (Garber 2011). The lighter weeks borrow from training science, where planned variation is called periodisation; session 3 explains why that idea is genuinely disputed.

Self-studyWhere the one-class skills plug in2 min▶

Every week of the arc is still a class, and a class needs its own shape: arrival, warm-up, the main work, cool-down, rest. Sequencing one class, including the peak-pose method, is taught in Yoga Theory session 7 ↗, and cueing in session 8 ↗. These pages assume you can do that and ask the question one level up: which poses go in which week. For the general craft of designing any course backwards from its outcome, see Course Design: backwards design ↗.

Real world

The beginner course that ends on its hardest night. Many studio beginner courses build to a "graduation" class in the last week with the most demanding sequence. It photographs well. It also means the final memory of the programme is the night some students could not keep up. Ending on consolidation, the poses people now own, done a little better, is the quieter choice and usually the one that brings people back.

Build-along

Juniper's programme charter

★ 22 min · everyone builds

A charter is one page that answers four questions before you choose a single pose: who it is for, what week 8 makes possible, what each phase does, and what each week has to hand to the next. Mei's one-line brief becomes Juniper's charter below (constructed); then you write one for a programme of your own.

Write who it is for, and who it is not for. One sentence each. "Not for" protects students: it is where you name the people who should start with a clinician or a different class.

★ Step 1 · Juniper: who (constructed)FOR: adults new to yoga who can get down to the floor and back up, with or without a chair to hold. NOT FOR, YET: anyone recovering from surgery or a recent injury, or told by a doctor to avoid exercise, until they have their clinician's go-ahead (session 2 intake).

Turn the brief into what week 8 makes possible. Mei's "feel confident coming to an open class" is a feeling. Make it observable: things a student can do without being talked through each step.

★ Step 2 · week 8 makes possibleBy week 8 a student can, at their own level: 1. move through a short standing sequence from memory 2. choose a regression for any pose that does not suit them 3. come down to the floor, rest, and get up safely 4. name two poses they will keep practising at home

Give each phase one job. Two weeks per phase for an 8-week programme. Write the job, not the poses.

★ Step 3 · four phasesWeeks 1-2 FOUNDATION standing, tabletop, first back bend, rest Weeks 3-4 BUILD one new demand at a time; week 4 lighter Weeks 5-6 INTEGRATE join poses into short flows; top load Weeks 7-8 CONSOLIDATE repeat and refine; week 7 lighter

Draw the chains. List the three or four poses you most want students to reach, then work backwards to what each needs first, the way the figure does. Every link must be taught in an earlier week.

★ Step 4 · Juniper's chainsTabletop (wk 1) -> Downward dog (wk 2) Tabletop (wk 1) -> Plank (wk 3) Low lunge (wk 3) -> High lunge (wk 5) -> Warrior I (wk 5) Warrior I + Tree -> Warrior III (wk 6) Sphinx (wk 1) -> Low cobra (wk 2), and Locust (wk 5)

Write the attendance rule into the charter. From Part 2: every new pose appears in at least two neighbouring weeks, so missing one class does not break a chain.

★ Step 5 · the repetition ruleEvery new pose is taught in 2 neighbouring weeks, then returns at least once more before week 8. Weeks 4 and 7 add nothing new.

Now write your own. Pick a real or imagined group: a workplace lunchtime class, a community hall for over-60s, a gym's beginner slot. Fill in the same five blocks. Keep it; session 2 adds the intake, session 3 the progressions, and session 4 runs your plan on the bench.

★ Step 6 · your charterFOR: ______________________ NOT FOR, YET: ____________ WEEK 8 MAKES POSSIBLE: 1 ______ 2 ______ 3 ______ PHASES: 1-2 ______ 3-4 ______ 5-6 ______ 7-8 ______ CHAINS: ______ -> ______ -> ______ REPETITION RULE: ______________________________

Resist choosing poses yet. Most people's first instinct is to open a pose list. The charter comes first because it tells you which poses are worth the weeks they cost. If a pose is not on a chain toward something in step 2, ask why it is in the programme.

Homework

Before session 2

20 min
  1. Finish your charter. All five blocks, on one page. If it runs to two pages, cut until it fits.
  2. Find a real programme description. A studio's beginner course page or a published class plan. Does it say how it progresses? Does it repeat anything? Count how many weeks it would survive a missed class.
  3. Test your week 8 list. For each item, could you watch a student and say yes or no? Rewrite any that you could not observe.
  4. List who might walk in. Write five different people who could plausibly book your programme. Session 2 turns that list into an intake.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 1 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · How did Sherman and colleagues (2011) structure their 12 weekly yoga classes?

"Six distinct and progressive classes were taught in pairs", using 17 relatively simple postures, with 20 minutes of home practice on non-class days. Repetition and gradual progression were both designed in.

2 · In Saper and colleagues' 2017 trial, what was the median number of the 12 yoga classes attended?

The median was 7 classes, and 44 percent reached the 9 classes set as adherent. Plan so that one missed week does not break a chain: teach each new pose twice, in neighbouring weeks.

3 · Which statement about the four-phase arc (foundation, build, integrate, consolidate) is accurate?

The trials show repetition and gradual progression, but rarely how; a 2021 review found only 5 percent of mind-body programmes reported a progression rule. The arc is a useful frame. Each week is still a class that needs its own sequencing.
