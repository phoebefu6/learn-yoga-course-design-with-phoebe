Part 1 · covers PAR-Q+ and Riebe 2015

Screen, then route

5 min live

The exercise world already has a screening tool, and it was built for exactly the person a yoga teacher is: someone who is not a clinician but needs to know who should check with one first. The tool does the asking. Your job is the routing: what happens next for each answer.

LiveThe PAR-Q+: seven questions, then a route3 min▶

The PAR-Q+ (Physical Activity Readiness Questionnaire for Everyone) is a pre-participation screen published by the PAR-Q+ Collaboration. A 2021 validation paper calls it "the international standard for pre-participation risk stratification and screening." The current official form is the 2025 edition, free at eparmedx.com ↗. Page 1 has seven questions. If someone answers yes to any of them, the form itself sends them to follow-up pages, and the electronic follow-up is called the ePARmed-X+.

The questions are reported to cover a heart condition or high blood pressure, chest pain, dizziness or loss of consciousness in the last 12 months, another chronic condition, prescribed medication, a bone, joint or soft-tissue problem, and being told to do only medically supervised activity. That wording is reported from copies of the form, so send your students to the official 2025 form and let them read it there, rather than retyping the questions into your own.

Notice what the form does not ask you to do. It does not ask the teacher to interpret anything. A yes routes to more questions or to a clinician; the teacher's part is to make sure the student has the form, and to hold their place while they follow it up.

Self-studyWhy ACSM made screening lighter in 20153 min▶

In 2015 the American College of Sports Medicine rewrote its pre-participation screening. Deborah Riebe and colleagues set out an algorithm built on three factors: "1) the individual's current level of physical activity, 2) presence of signs or symptoms and/or known cardiovascular, metabolic, or renal disease, and 3) desired exercise intensity." Signs, symptoms and known disease are one factor, not two. Counting risk factors such as age or cholesterol was dropped from the process.

The reason is the part a programme designer should remember. The old approach "can result in excessive physician referrals, possibly creating a barrier to exercise participation." Sending everyone to a doctor is not the safe option; it is an option that keeps people out of a class that would probably have done them good. Screening exists to find the few who need a check, and to let everyone else start.

For a gentle beginner programme, two of the three factors are already known: the desired intensity is low and the students are mostly inactive. That leaves the middle factor, which is precisely the one PAR-Q+ asks about. If you want the general method behind intake as needs analysis, see Course Design: needs analysis ↗.

Real world

The waiver that asks everything and routes nothing. A common studio sign-up form lists twenty conditions to tick, then asks for a signature. Nobody reads the ticks before class, and the student who ticked "high blood pressure" is never told what to do about it. A short form with a clear route for each answer protects people better than a long form nobody acts on.

Part 2 · covers Jasien 2015, Swain and McGwin 2016, Cramer 2015

A flag is a referral, not a diagnosis

5 min live

Some answers do not stop anyone joining but still change your plan: sore wrists, a knee that dislikes kneeling, a doctor's advice to keep the head above the heart. Those become caution flags. A flag is a short tag that says "prepare a regression and keep the referral door open". Three studies show how to read the evidence behind flags honestly, without overstating it in either direction.

LiveHead-down poses and the eye: read the small print3 min▶

Jessica Jasien and colleagues measured eye pressure in 10 people with glaucoma and 10 without, during 2-minute holds of four poses. In downward dog, pressure in the glaucoma group went from 17 to 28 mmHg (17 to 29 in the normal group). Standing forward fold: 17 to 27 (18 to 26). Plough: 18 to 24 (18 to 22). Legs up the wall: 17 to 21 in both. Pressure was back to baseline within 2 minutes of sitting up.

The honest reading has two halves. Yes, head-down poses raise eye pressure quickly, and the rise was similar with or without glaucoma. No, the study did not test whether that rise makes glaucoma progress: 20 people, one session. So a teacher neither says "inversions are dangerous for glaucoma" nor "it is fine". A student who says their eye doctor advised them to avoid head-below-heart positions gets a "head below heart" flag and a prepared alternative, and the question of risk stays with the eye doctor.

Self-studyInjury rates by age, and what "as safe as" means3 min▶

Thomas Swain and Gerald McGwin estimated 29,590 yoga injuries treated in US emergency departments from 2001 to 2014. In 2014 the rate per 100,000 participants was 57.9 at age 65 and over, 17.7 at 45 to 64, and 11.9 at 18 to 44. The trunk accounted for 46.6 percent of injuries, and sprains and strains for 45.0 percent.

The honest reading: the rate for older participants was several times the rate for younger adults, which is a reason to plan gentle progressions and prepared regressions for a mixed-age room. It is not a reason to treat a 70-year-old as fragile, and these are emergency visits counted nationally, not a measure of any one class or style.

Holger Cramer and colleagues reviewed 301 randomised yoga trials. Only 94 of them, with 8,430 participants, reported adverse events at all, and in those there was no difference between yoga and usual care or exercise: "yoga appears as safe as usual care and exercise." Read both halves again. "As safe as" exercise is not "risk free", and two thirds of trials did not report adverse events, so the evidence is thinner than the headline. Flags and regressions are how a programme respects both facts.

Self-studyWhen to send someone to a professional now2 min▶

Some things a student mentions are not flags to plan around; they are reasons to stop and refer. The NHS back pain page (reviewed 5 March 2026) is a useful plain-language list. See a GP if pain does not improve after a few weeks, is worse at night, or came with unexplained weight loss. Urgent GP or 111 if severe pain starts suddenly or gets worse quickly, or the person feels hot, shivery or unwell. Call 999 or go to A&E for numbness, tingling or weakness in both legs, loss of feeling around the genitals or anus, bladder or bowel changes, chest pain, or pain after a serious accident. Session 6 returns to this list when the programme ends.

Real world

"I read that inversions are bad for glaucoma." A familiar pattern: a teacher hears a student has an eye condition and announces the student must never do downward dog. The intention is kind; the act is a diagnosis and a prescription in one sentence. The scope-safe version is a question and an offer: "Has your eye doctor said anything about head-down positions? Either way, here is the version with hands on a chair seat."

Part 3 · covers Yoga Alliance Scope of Practice and Yoga Therapy Policy

Your scope, and consent

5 min live

The intake gives you information. Scope decides what you are allowed to do with it. The line is simpler than most new teachers fear: you ask, adapt, refer and record. You never diagnose, treat or clear someone for exercise.

LiveWhat Yoga Alliance actually says3 min▶

Yoga Alliance's Scope of Practice (updated 27 February 2020) frames the line as advice and credentials: "Members must not provide advice or services for which they are not properly and currently licensed." Unless a teacher is competent and credentialed in the field, the options are to gain the credentials, refer the student to a qualified professional, or decline. Members may keep a referral list of local resources, including medical and mental health professionals.

A correction worth knowing, because it is often misquoted: the Scope of Practice page does not use the words "diagnose" or "treat". That wording is in a separate document, the Yoga Therapy Policy (updated 18 November 2021), where Yoga Alliance says it does "not credential yoga therapy techniques, including the diagnosis or treatment" of an injury or illness. Yoga therapy is its own credential. A 200-hour registration does not make anyone a yoga therapist.

Self-studyConsent for touch, and keeping answers private3 min▶

The same Scope of Practice page sets the rule for hands-on adjustments: only with "Explicit and Informed Consent". Two lines settle most arguments: "Previous consent does not imply future permission", and "Silence or lack of resistance, in and of itself, does not demonstrate consent." So the intake question "hands-on adjustments: yes or no" is a starting point, not a standing permission. Ask again in the room, every time, and make "no" as easy to say as "yes".

Intake answers are personal. The practical norm: collect only what changes your plan, keep it where only you (and the owner, if needed) can see it, and do not keep it longer than the programme needs. Privacy and data-protection rules differ by country, so check what applies where you teach; this is a working habit, not a legal checklist.

Real world

The adjustment that was fine last week. A student said yes to hands-on help in week 2. In week 5 she has a sore shoulder and does not mention it. A teacher who treats the week 2 yes as permanent finds out the hard way. Teachers who ask quietly each time ("would a hand on your back help here?") rarely have that conversation at all.

Build-along

Juniper's intake and flag map

★ 22 min · everyone builds

Juniper's twelve students (constructed) have filled in an intake. Five raised nothing to plan around. Three find weight on the hands uncomfortable, two dislike kneeling or deep bending, one is unsteady on one leg, and one was told to go easy on deep back bends. Nobody has a head-below-heart flag yet. The map below turns those flags into a plan: which poses in Juniper's wave plan carry each flag, and the regression that is ready for each.

Write a short intake. Five questions of your own, plus the link to the official health screen. Ask only what would change your plan.

★ Step 1 · Juniper's intake (constructed)Before week 1, please complete the PAR-Q+ (2025 edition) at eparmedx.com. If you answer YES to any question, follow the form's next steps before your first class. 1. How active are you now? (most days / some days / rarely) 2. Has a doctor advised you to avoid any movement or position? (optional: what, in your own words) 3. Any pain or injury right now? (yes / no; where, if yes) 4. What do you hope to be able to do by week 8? 5. Hands-on adjustments: yes / no (you can change your answer at any class)

Write the "a yes" script. What you say, by email or at the door, to someone who answered yes on the PAR-Q+. It refers, keeps the place open, and diagnoses nothing.

★ Step 2 · the referral script"Thanks for filling this in. Because you answered yes on the health form, please follow its next steps or check with your doctor before we start. Your place is held. When you are ready, just let me know anything they suggested and I will have options ready for you." Never: "That should be fine." / "You can't do yoga."

List the flags. One short tag per student, in plain words, with no condition names. A tag describes what to plan for, not what is wrong.

★ Step 3 · Juniper's flags (constructed)wrist 3 weight on the hands is uncomfortable knee 2 kneeling or deep bending is uncomfortable balance 1 unsteady on one leg back 1 told to go easy on deep back bends and loaded folds headdown 0 (none for now; keep the column) none 5

Map flags to the poses in your plan. Go through the wave plan pose by pose and write which flags each one carries. Some carry two.

★ Step 4 · flag -> poseswrist -> Tabletop, Cat-cow, Downward dog, Plank knee -> Chair, Tabletop, Cat-cow, Low lunge, Pigeon balance -> Tree, High lunge, Warrior III back -> Low cobra, Locust headdown -> Downward dog

Write each regression. One line per pose, using props you actually have. Offer it to the whole room as an option, so nobody is singled out. Pose mechanics belong to Yoga Theory session 6 ↗; here you only choose and record.

★ Step 5 · prepared regressionsTabletop forearms on blocks, pad under the knees Cat-cow seated cat-cow, hands on the knees Downward dog hands on a wall or a chair seat Plank plank against a wall Chair shallow bend, back against a wall Low lunge pad under the back knee, or a short standing lunge Pigeon lying figure four Tree toes on the floor, hand on a wall High lunge back heel down, one hand on a wall Warrior III hands on a wall, standing leg soft Low cobra sphinx, small range Locust one leg at a time, chest down

Now do your own. Take the five people you listed after session 1. Give each a plausible flag or none, map them to the poses in your charter, and write the regressions. Session 4's bench counts every one of these as a "regression offer": in Juniper's wave plan it counts 119 across the 8 weeks.

Keep the empty column. Juniper has no head-below-heart flag today. Someone may join in week 5 with one. Because the column exists and Downward dog already has a regression, that student costs you one sentence, not a replanned week. Session 4's bench tests exactly that.

Homework

Before session 3

20 min
  1. Read the real form. Open the 2025 PAR-Q+ at eparmedx.com and read all seven questions and the follow-up instructions. Note one thing it asks that your own intake did not.
  2. Say the script aloud. Practise the step 2 referral script until it sounds like you. Check it contains no promise and no verdict.
  3. Find your local referral list. Two or three local professionals or services you could point a student to. Keep it with your intake.
  4. Test your regressions with props. For each regression in step 5, check you own the prop it needs and can set it up in under a minute.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 2 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · A student answers yes to one of the seven PAR-Q+ page 1 questions. What is the teacher's job?

Any yes on page 1 routes to follow-up (the ePARmed-X+) or a clinician. The teacher routes and keeps the door open; the teacher never clears anyone for exercise. The 2015 ACSM update made screening lighter precisely because over-referral was a barrier, so routing the few with a yes matters more than sending everyone.

2 · Jasien and colleagues (2015) measured eye pressure in head-down poses. What is the honest reading?

In downward dog pressure went from 17 to 28 mmHg with glaucoma and 17 to 29 without, in a study of 20 people. Progression risk was not tested, so a "head below heart" flag comes from the student's eye doctor, never from the teacher's reading of a paper.

3 · A student agreed to hands-on adjustments in week 2. What does that mean in week 5?

Yoga Alliance's Scope of Practice requires explicit and informed consent for touch: "Previous consent does not imply future permission", and silence or lack of resistance does not demonstrate consent.
