- reported: only secondary sources were seen.
- could not verify: no readable source was reached; do not cite the number.

## 1. Multi-week yoga programmes in trials

### 1a. Sherman KJ et al 2011 - yoga vs stretching vs self-care book
Citation: Sherman KJ, Cherkin DC, Wellman RD, Cook AJ, Hawkes RJ, Delaney K, Deyo RA. A randomized trial comparing yoga, stretching, and a self-care book for chronic low back pain. Arch Intern Med. 2011;171(22):2019-2026. doi:10.1001/archinternmed.2011.524. PMID 22025101. PMCID PMC3279296.
Tier: read-at-source (abstract + PMC full text).

- Sample: 228 adults; yoga 92, stretching 91, self-care book 45 (2:2:1 randomisation). Quote: "228 adults with chronic low back pain were randomized to 12 weekly classes of yoga (92 patients)".
- Class length and home practice: "A series of 12 standardized weekly 75-minute yoga and stretching classes" ... "asked to practice 20 minutes on non-class days".
- Structure (progressive, paired): viniyoga, 17 relatively simple postures. Quote: "Six distinct and progressive classes were taught in pairs." Each class: breathing, 5 to 11 postures (about 45-50 min), guided deep relaxation.
- Teachers: at least 500 hours viniyoga training and five years teaching experience.
- 12-week results vs self-care: function mean difference -2.5 (95% CI -3.7 to -1.3) on the 23-point modified Roland scale; symptoms -1.1 (95% CI -1.7 to -0.4). At 26 weeks function still -1.8 (95% CI -3.1 to -0.5).
- Key nuance: "Yoga was not superior to conventional stretching exercises at any time point."
- Adherence: "proportion attending at least 8 classes (65% for yoga and 59% for stretching)"; median classes attended among those attending at least one: 10 (yoga) vs 9 (stretching).
- Home practice: median weekly practice in yoga group 100 min at week 6, 60 min at week 12; at 26 weeks 59% of yoga group practised at least 3 days in prior week (median 35 min/week).

Design lesson: a fixed, manualised, progressive sequence (6 class plans each taught twice) plus daily home practice; home practice decays across the programme.

### 1b. Tilbrook HE et al 2011 - 12-week yoga programme, UK
Citation: Tilbrook HE, Cox H, Hewitt CE, Kang'ombe AR, Chuang LH, Jayakody S, Aplin JD, Semlyen A, Trewhela A, Watt I, Torgerson DJ. Yoga for chronic low back pain: a randomized trial. Ann Intern Med. 2011;155(9):569-578. doi:10.7326/0003-4819-155-9-201111010-00003. PMID 22041945.
Tier: read-at-source (abstract only; full text not read, so class length and home-practice details are not verified here).

- Sample: 313 adults; yoga n = 156, usual care n = 157; 13 non-NHS premises.
- Structure: "a 12-class, gradually progressing yoga program delivered by 12 teachers over 3 months."
- Attendance: "93 (60%) patients offered yoga attended at least 3 of the first 6 sessions and at least 3 other sessions."
- RMDQ difference (yoga lower = better): 2.17 points (95% CI 1.03 to 3.31) at 3 months; 1.48 (0.33 to 2.62) at 6 months; 1.57 (0.42 to 2.71) at 12 months.
- Pain and general health scores were similar between groups at all time points.
- Adverse events: 12 of 156 yoga vs 2 of 157 usual care, "mostly increased pain".
- Limitation: missing primary outcome data (yoga 21, usual care 18).
- Class length (often stated as 75 minutes) and home-practice CD: reported only; not read at source in this pass.

### 1c. Saper RB et al 2017 - yoga vs physical therapy vs education, noninferiority
Citation: Saper RB, Lemaster C, Delitto A, Sherman KJ, Herman PM, Sadikova E, Stevans J, Keosaian JE, Cerrada CJ, Femia AL, Roseen EJ, Gardiner P, Gergen Barnett K, Faulkner C, Weinberg J. Yoga, physical therapy, or education for chronic low back pain: a randomized noninferiority trial. Ann Intern Med. 2017;167(2):85-94. doi:10.7326/M16-2579. PMID 28631003. PMCID PMC6392183.
Tier: read-at-source (abstract + PMC full text).

- Sample: 320 predominantly low-income, racially diverse adults; yoga n = 127, PT n = 129, education n = 64.
- Dose: "12 weekly yoga classes, 15 PT visits, or an educational book and newsletters." Classes were 75 minutes ("12 weekly 75-minute classes").
- Structure: manualised; each class began with relaxation/meditation, breathing and philosophy, then poses, then relaxation. "Pose variations and aids (such as chair, strap, and blocks) accommodated various abilities." Participant to instructor ratio under 5:1. Instructors got 8 hours of study training.
- Home practice: "Thirty minutes of daily home practice, facilitated by a DVD, a manual, and take-home yoga supplies, was strongly encouraged."
- Noninferiority: margins 1.5 (RMDQ) and 1.0 (pain). "One-sided 95% lower confidence limits were 0.83 (RMDQ) and 0.97 (pain), demonstrating noninferiority of yoga to PT." Yoga was not superior to education.
- Medication: yoga and PT participants 21 and 22 percentage points less likely than education to use pain medication at 12 weeks.
- Attendance: "median yoga attendance was 7 classes (interquartile range, 3 to 10)." Adherent (at least 9 of 12 classes) a priori: 56 yoga participants (44%).
- Home practice reported by 95 yoga participants (75%); median 27 minutes (IQR 17 to 35), 4 days per week.
- Maintenance phase: drop-in classes vs home practice made no difference to RMDQ or pain at 52 weeks.
- Authors compare: "Our yoga participants attended a median of 7 classes compared with 10 in Sherman and colleagues' trial."

Cross-trial design notes (synthesis, not a quote): all three used 12 weekly group classes, a fixed manualised sequence that progressed gradually, prop-based variations, and prescribed home practice. Realistic attendance is 60-67% meeting a "most classes" threshold in well-resourced samples, and median 7 of 12 in a low-income sample.

## 2. Safety

### 2a. Cramer H et al 2015 - safety of yoga, SR and meta-analysis of RCTs
Citation: Cramer H, Ward L, Saper R, Fishbein D, Dobos G, Lauche R. The safety of yoga: a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials. Am J Epidemiol. 2015;182(4):281-293. doi:10.1093/aje/kwv071. PMID 26116216.
Tier: read-at-source (abstract).

- Scope: "Of 301 identified randomized controlled trials of yoga, 94 (1975-2014; total of 8,430 participants) reported on adverse events."
- vs usual care or exercise: "No differences in the frequency of intervention-related, nonserious, or serious adverse events and of dropouts due to adverse events".
- vs psychological/educational controls: more intervention-related AEs (OR 4.21, 95% CI 1.01 to 17.67) and more nonserious AEs (OR 7.30, 95% CI 1.91 to 27.92); serious AEs comparable.
- Conclusion: "yoga appears as safe as usual care and exercise."
- Caveat for the course: only 94 of 301 trials reported AEs at all, so "safe" rests on under-reported data.

### 2b. Swain TA, McGwin G 2016 - yoga injuries in US emergency departments
Citation: Swain TA, McGwin G. Yoga-related injuries in the United States from 2001 to 2014. Orthop J Sports Med. 2016;4(11):2325967116671703. doi:10.1177/2325967116671703. PMID 27896293. PMCID PMC5117171.
Tier: read-at-source (abstract).

- Data: NEISS emergency department sample, scaled with National Health Statistics Reports participation estimates.
- Count: "There were 29,590 yoga-related injuries seen in hospital emergency departments from 2001 to 2014."
- Type: trunk 46.6% of injuries; sprain/strain 45.0% of diagnoses.
- Age 65+ finding (2014 rates per 100,000 participants): 65 and older 57.9; 18-44 11.9; 45-64 17.7.
- The overall injury rate increased from 2001 to 2014.
- Note: these are national estimates from a weighted sample, so 29,590 is an estimate, not a raw count.

### 2c. Jasien JV et al 2015 - intraocular pressure in head-down poses
Citation: Jasien JV, Jonas JB, de Moraes CG, Ritch R. Intraocular pressure rise in subjects with and without glaucoma during four common yoga positions. PLoS One. 2015;10(12):e0144505. doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0144505. PMID 26698309. PMCID PMC4689525.
Tier: read-at-source (abstract).

- Sample: 10 with primary open-angle glaucoma, 10 normal; each pose held two minutes.
- Poses: Adho Mukha Svanasana (downward dog), Uttanasana (standing forward fold), Halasana (plough), Viparita Karani (legs up the wall).
- Largest rise, downward dog: "IOP increase from 17 +/- 3.2 mmHg to 28 +/- 3.8 mmHg in glaucoma patients" (normal: 17 to 29).
- Uttanasana: glaucoma 17 to 27; normal 18 to 26. Halasana: glaucoma 18 to 24; normal 18 to 22. Viparita Karani: glaucoma 17 to 21; normal 17 to 21 (all mmHg).
- Recovery: IOP back to baseline within two minutes of sitting up.
- Glaucoma vs normal rise not significantly different (P = 0.813).
- Caveat: n = 20, observational; whether this raises progression risk was not tested.

## 3. Preparticipation screening

### 3a. PAR-Q+ (Physical Activity Readiness Questionnaire for Everyone)
Official site: https://eparmedx.com/ (read 2026-10-12). Tier: read-at-source for the site claims below.
- What it is: a pre-participation screening tool to "help determine readiness for increased physical activity" (eparmedx.com).
- Questions: page 1 has "the seven (7) evidence-based questions" (eparmedx.com). Any YES routes the person to follow-up pages; the electronic follow-up is ePARmed-X+.
- Current version: the fillable PDF on eparmedx.com is labelled 2025 (copyright "PAR-Q+ Collaboration"). A 2020 version existed but the current official form is the 2025 edition; teach the current one.
- Wording of the 7 questions (heart condition or high blood pressure; chest pain; dizziness or loss of consciousness in last 12 months; other chronic condition; prescribed medication; bone, joint or soft-tissue problem; told to do only medically supervised activity): reported via third-party copies of the 2025 form found by search, not read on the official PDF in this pass. The same copies state clearance is valid for a maximum of 12 months (reported).
- Developers: the site names only the PAR-Q+ Collaboration. Supporting paper (citation only, not read in full): Bredin SS, Gledhill N, Jamnik VK, Warburton DE. PAR-Q+ and ePARmed-X+: new risk stratification and physical activity clearance strategy for physicians and patients alike. Can Fam Physician. 2013;59(3):273-277. PMID 23486800. PMCID PMC3596208. Tier: reported.
- Status claim, read-at-source in a validation abstract (Schwartz J ... Warburton DER. Front Cardiovasc Med. 2021;8:712696. doi:10.3389/fcvm.2021.712696. PMID 34381827): PAR-Q+ "is the international standard for pre-participation risk stratification and screening."

### 3b. Riebe D et al 2015 - ACSM updated preparticipation screening
Citation: Riebe D, Franklin BA, Thompson PD, Garber CE, Whitfield GP, Magal M, Pescatello LS. Updating ACSM's recommendations for exercise preparticipation health screening. Med Sci Sports Exerc. 2015;47(11):2473-2479. doi:10.1249/MSS.0000000000000664. PMID 26473759. (Erratum: Med Sci Sports Exerc. 2016;48(3):579.)
Tier: read-at-source (abstract).

- CORRECTION to the brief: the three factors as written in the abstract are (1) current physical activity, (2) signs/symptoms and/or known cardiovascular, metabolic or renal disease, (3) DESIRED EXERCISE INTENSITY. "Known disease" and "signs/symptoms" are one combined factor, not two. Quote: "1) the individual's current level of physical activity, 2) presence of signs or symptoms and/or known cardiovascular, metabolic, or renal disease, and 3) desired exercise intensity".
- Why it changed: old screening "can result in excessive physician referrals, possibly creating a barrier to exercise participation."
- Risk-factor profiling dropped: "risk factor profiling is no longer included in the exercise preparticipation health screening process."
- Purpose: identify people at elevated risk of exercise-related sudden cardiac death and/or acute myocardial infarction.

## 4. Progression

### 4a. ACSM 2009 position stand - progression models in resistance training
Citation: American College of Sports Medicine. American College of Sports Medicine position stand. Progression models in resistance training for healthy adults. Med Sci Sports Exerc. 2009;41(3):687-708. doi:10.1249/MSS.0b013e3181915670. PMID 19204579.
Tier: read-at-source (abstract). The formal definition of "progressive overload" sits in the full text, which was not read; do not quote a definition from this source.

- 2-10% rule: "recommended that 2-10% increase in load be applied when the individual can perform the current workload for one to two repetitions over the desired number."
- Novice: loads at 8-12 RM; frequency "2-3 d x wk(-1) for novice training".
- Caveat in the abstract: recommendations "should be applied in context and should be contingent upon an individual's target goals, physical capacity, and training status."
- Course use: the principle (increase only after the current load is mastered, in small steps) transfers to yoga as hold time, pose variation or sequence complexity; the 2-10% number is for external load and does not transfer directly to bodyweight poses. That transfer is our inference, not ACSM's.

### 4b. Garber CE et al 2011 - ACSM quantity and quality of exercise
Citation: Garber CE, Blissmer B, Deschenes MR, Franklin BA, Lamonte MJ, Lee IM, Nieman DC, Swain DP; American College of Sports Medicine. American College of Sports Medicine position stand. Quantity and quality of exercise for developing and maintaining cardiorespiratory, musculoskeletal, and neuromotor fitness in apparently healthy adults: guidance for prescribing exercise. Med Sci Sports Exerc. 2011;43(7):1334-1359. doi:10.1249/MSS.0b013e318213fefb. PMID 21694556.
Tier: read-at-source (abstract) for the lines below; full text paywalled (HTTP 402) so table detail is reported.

- Flexibility (abstract): "a series of flexibility exercises for each the major muscle-tendon groups (a total of 60 s per exercise) on >=2 d/wk is recommended."
- Neuromotor (abstract): on 2-3 d/wk adults should perform "neuromotor exercise involving balance, agility, and coordination."
- Hold 10-30 s for most adults, 30-60 s may benefit older adults: reported only (secondary handouts and ACSM blog summaries attribute it to Garber 2011 / ACSM GETP). Not read in the position stand itself in this pass.
- Neuromotor 20-30 min per day and "yoga, tai chi" as examples: reported only.
- Adherence line in abstract: "supervision by an experienced fitness instructor, and exercise that is pleasant and enjoyable can improve adoption and adherence".
- Safety line: "gradual progression of exercise intensity and volume may reduce the risks of exercise."

## 5. Periodisation and the dispute

### 5a. Williams TD et al 2017 - periodised vs non-periodised strength, meta-analysis
Citation: Williams TD, Tolusso DV, Fedewa MV, Esco MR. Comparison of periodized and non-periodized resistance training on maximal strength: a meta-analysis. Sports Med. 2017;47(10):2083-2100. doi:10.1007/s40279-017-0734-y. PMID 28497285.
Tier: read-at-source (abstract).

- Data: 81 effects from 18 studies (1988-2015), 1RM squat, bench or leg press.
- Effect: "periodized resistance training was greater than non-periodized resistance training (ES = 0.43, 95% CI 0.27-0.58; P < 0.001)."
- Moderators: undulating models more favourable; gains greater in untrained; higher frequency and longer studies associated with larger gains.
- Two critical comments were published (Sports Med 2018;48(2):491-494 and 495-496); not read.

### 5b. Afonso J et al 2019 - systematic review of the meta-analyses (the critique)
Citation: Afonso J, Rocha T, Nikolaidis PT, Clemente FM, Rosemann T, Knechtle B. A systematic review of meta-analyses comparing periodized and non-periodized exercise programs: why we should go back to original research. Front Physiol. 2019;10:1023. doi:10.3389/fphys.2019.01023. PMID 31440169. PMCID PMC6692867.
Tier: read-at-source (abstract). (A 2017 Afonso critique was searched for and not located; this 2019 paper is the citable one.)

- Core finding: "None of the 21 studies included in the two meta-analyses compared periodized programs with varied, non-periodized programs."
- Timing predictions untested: "The accuracy of the predictions concerning the proposed timings of adaptations was not scrutinized by any of the 21 studies."
- Scope: strength training only.
- Implication: the evidence supports VARIATION, not necessarily PERIODISED variation (the comparison group was usually constant training).

Related: Afonso J, Clemente FM, Ribeiro J, Ferreira M, Fernandes RJ. Towards a de facto nonlinear periodization: extending nonlinearity from programming to periodizing. Sports (Basel). 2020;8(8):110. doi:10.3390/sports8080110. PMID 32784454. Read-at-source (abstract): linear and nonlinear "have not been clearly defined in the literature".

### 5c. Kiely J 2012 and 2018 - the conceptual critique
Citation 1: Kiely J. Periodization paradigms in the 21st century: evidence-led or tradition-driven? Int J Sports Physiol Perform. 2012;7(3):242-250. doi:10.1123/ijspp.7.3.242.
Tier: read-at-source (abstract via Crossref).
- "substantial evidence has emerged demonstrating that training responses vary extensively, depending upon multiple underlying factors."
- Not a rejection: "not to suggest a whole-scale rejection of periodization theories but to promote a refined awareness".
- Recommends "sensitive and responsive training systems".

Citation 2: Kiely J. Periodization theory: confronting an inconvenient truth. Sports Med. 2018;48(4):753-764. doi:10.1007/s40279-017-0823-y. PMID 29189930. PMCID PMC5856877.
Tier: read-at-source (abstract).
- Argument: periodisation rests on Selye's stress model, which has since evolved; "if the original scientific platform upon which periodization theory was founded has disintegrated, should we critically re-evaluate".
- No effect sizes (conceptual paper).

Coach survey (context): Anyadike-Danes K, Donath L, Kiely J. Coaches' perceptions of common planning concepts within training theory: an international survey. Sports Med Open. 2023;9(1):109. doi:10.1186/s40798-023-00657-6. PMID 37989900. Read-at-source (abstract): n = 106 coaches; 71% called their approach periodisation, but only 33% agreed adaptations are achievable within fixed timeframes and 10% supported fixed targets.

Course takeaway (synthesis): plan a multi-week arc, but treat week-by-week targets as adjustable to the group in front of you.

## 6. Motor learning

### 6a. Shea CH, Lai Q, Black C, Park JH 2000 - spacing across days
Citation: Shea CH, Lai Q, Black C, Park JH. Spacing practice sessions across days benefits the learning of motor skills. Hum Mov Sci. 2000;19(5):737-760. doi:10.1016/S0167-9457(00)00021-X.
Tier: could not verify (citation confirmed via Crossref; abstract behind ScienceDirect 403 and not in OpenAlex). Do not cite numbers from it; the title itself states the direction of the finding.

### 6b. Contextual interference: blocked vs random practice
Shea JB, Morgan RL. Contextual interference effects on the acquisition, retention, and transfer of a motor skill. J Exp Psychol Hum Learn Mem. 1979;5(2):179-187. doi:10.1037/0278-7393.5.2.179.
Tier: read-at-source (abstract via OpenAlex).
- Design: three motor tasks, blocked (low interference) or random (high interference); retention after 10 min or 10 days.
- Finding: "retention was greater following high interference (random) acquisition than after low interference (blocked) acquisition when retention was measured under changed contextual interference conditions."
- Transfer also greater after random practice, most notably for the most complex transfer task.
- The "blocked is better during acquisition" half is not stated in this abstract; it is reported in reviews (Magill and Hall 1990) and not read here.

Magill RA, Hall KG. A review of the contextual interference effect in motor skill acquisition. Hum Mov Sci. 1990;9(3-5):241-289. doi:10.1016/0167-9457(90)90005-X.
Tier: could not verify (abstract not reachable). Citation confirmed via Crossref.

Brady F. Contextual interference: a meta-analytic study. Percept Mot Skills. 2004;99(1):116-126. doi:10.2466/pms.99.1.116-126. PMID 15446636.
Tier: read-at-source (abstract).
- "139 estimates of effect sizes from 61 studies. The average overall effect size was .38."
- Basic research .57 vs applied research .19; adults .50 vs younger learners .10.

Brady F. The contextual interference effect and sport skills. Percept Mot Skills. 2008;106(2):461-472. doi:10.2466/pms.106.2.461-472. PMID 18556902.
Tier: read-at-source (abstract).
- The effect is "relatively robust in basic research, but considerably weaker in applied settings."

Novice caveat (link to 6c): the applied-settings and younger-learner effects are small, and the challenge point framework predicts that low-interference (blocked) practice suits beginners on difficult tasks. For a beginner yoga programme: block new poses first, then interleave once they are familiar. This sequencing is an inference from 6b and 6c, not a tested yoga finding.

### 6c. Guadagnoli MA, Lee TD 2004 - challenge point framework
Citation: Guadagnoli MA, Lee TD. Challenge point: a framework for conceptualizing the effects of various practice conditions in motor learning. J Mot Behav. 2004;36(2):212-224. doi:10.3200/JMBR.36.2.212-224. PMID 15130871.
Tier: read-at-source (abstract).
- "motor tasks represent different challenges for performers of different abilities."
- Learning is related to information arising from performance, "optimized along functions relating the difficulty of the task to the skill level of the performer."
- The abstract is theoretical; it proposes testable hypotheses and reports no effect sizes.

## 7. Stretching and flexibility gains over weeks

### 7a. Thomas E et al 2018 - stretching typology and duration
Citation: Thomas E, Bianco A, Paoli A, Palma A. The relation between stretching typology and stretching duration: the effects on range of motion. Int J Sports Med. 2018;39(4):243-254. doi:10.1055/s-0044-101146. PMID 29506306.
Tier: read-at-source (abstract).
- 23 articles in the quantitative synthesis; all typologies improved ROM long term, static gained more than ballistic or PNF.
- Weekly volume: "Time spent stretching per week seems fundamental to elicit range of movement improvements when stretches are applied for at least or more than 5 min".
- Per session time "does not seem to have significant effects for ROM gains"; weekly frequency positively associated.
- Recommendation: "at least 5 days a week for at least 5 min per week using static stretching".

### 7b. Konrad A et al 2024 (2023 online) - chronic stretching meta-analysis (the counterpoint)
Citation: Konrad A, Alizadeh S, Daneshjoo A, Anvar SH, Graham A, Zahiri A, Goudini R, Edwards C, Scharf C, Behm DG. Chronic effects of stretching on range of motion with consideration of potential moderating variables: a systematic review with meta-analysis. J Sport Health Sci. 2024;13(2):186-194. doi:10.1016/j.jshs.2023.06.002. PMID 37301370. PMCID PMC10980866.
Tier: read-at-source (abstract).
- 77 studies, 186 effect sizes. Overall "effect size = -1.002 ... 95% confidence interval: -1.165 to -0.840" (moderate, favouring stretching; negative sign is the paper's convention).
- PNF and static beat ballistic/dynamic; females gained more than males.
- Key dispute with 7a: "neither volume, intensity, nor frequency of stretching were found to play a significant role in ROM yields."

### 7c. Konrad A et al 2024 - static stretching vs foam rolling (how fast)
Citation: Konrad A, Alizadeh S, Anvar SH, Fischer J, Manieu J, Behm DG. Static stretch training versus foam rolling training effects on range of motion: a systematic review and meta-analysis. Sports Med. 2024;54(9):2311-2326. doi:10.1007/s40279-024-02041-0. PMID 38760635. PMCID PMC11393112.
Tier: read-at-source (abstract).
- 85 studies (72 static stretching), 204 effect sizes. Static stretching ES -1.006, foam rolling ES -0.729 vs control.
- Short programmes: over 4 weeks or less, static stretching ES -1.436 (significant) vs foam rolling -0.229 (not significant). So measurable ROM change within about 4 weeks is supported for static stretching.

### 7d. Related reviews (read-at-source, abstracts)
- Behm DG, Alizadeh S, ... Konrad A. Acute effects of various stretching techniques on range of motion: a systematic review with meta-analysis. Sports Med Open. 2023;9(1):107. doi:10.1186/s40798-023-00652-x. PMID 37962709. 47 studies; a single bout gives "acute small magnitude ROM improvements"; GRADE moderate confidence.
- Alizadeh S, ... Konrad A, Behm DG. Resistance training induces improvements in range of motion: a systematic review and meta-analysis. Sports Med. 2023;53(3):707-722. doi:10.1007/s40279-022-01804-x. PMID 36622555. 55 studies; RT ES 0.73 for ROM; no difference vs stretch training (ES 0.08); but no significant ROM gain from bodyweight-only RT.
- Arntz F, ... Chaabene H. Chronic effects of static stretching exercises on muscle strength and power in healthy individuals across the lifespan. Sports Med. 2023;53(3):723-745. doi:10.1007/s40279-022-01806-9. PMID 36719536. 41 studies; flexibility SMD 0.96 (95% CI 0.70 to 1.22); more repetitions and more time under stretch per session associated with larger flexibility gains (contradicting Thomas 2018 on per-session time).

Course takeaway (synthesis): ROM gains from regular static stretching are moderate and can appear within about 4 weeks; whether weekly volume matters is disputed across meta-analyses, so do not promise a dose-response.

## 8. Mixed-level rooms

### 8a. Deunk MI et al 2018 - differentiation practices, primary education
Citation: Deunk MI, Smale-Jacobse AE, de Boer H, Doolaard S, Bosker RJ. Effective differentiation practices: a systematic review and meta-analysis of studies on the cognitive effects of differentiation practices in primary education. Educ Res Rev. 2018;24:31-54. doi:10.1016/j.edurev.2018.02.002.
Tier: read-at-source (abstract via OpenAlex); per-practice effect sizes are in the full text and were not read.
- Scope: 21 studies since 1995, 78 effect sizes, language and maths.
- Computerised differentiation and differentiation within broader programmes: "small to moderate positive effects".
- Homogeneous ability grouping: "small negative effect on low-ability students, but no effect on others."
- Message: "homogeneous ability grouping alone is not enough to guarantee differentiated instruction."

### 8b. Smale-Jacobse AE et al 2019 - differentiated instruction, secondary education
Citation: Smale-Jacobse AE, Meijer A, Helms-Lorenz M, Maulana R. Differentiated instruction in secondary education: a systematic review of research evidence. Front Psychol. 2019;10:2366. doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2019.02366. PMID 31824362. PMCID PMC6883934.
Tier: read-at-source (abstract).
- Only 14 papers on 12 unique studies (2006-2016) qualified.
- "Summarized effect sizes across studies range from d = +0.741 to +0.509 (omitting an outlier)."
- Limited/mixed message: "there are still severe knowledge gaps. More research is needed before drawing convincing conclusions".
- Caveat: both reviews are school academic outcomes, not movement classes; transfer to a yoga room is an inference.

### 8c. CAST Universal Design for Learning (UDL) Guidelines
Source: https://udlguidelines.cast.org/ (read 2026-10-12). Tier: read-at-source.
- Current version: 3.0, released by CAST on July 30, 2024.
- Structure: three principles (Engagement; Representation; Action and Expression), nine guidelines (three per principle), 36 considerations in total.
- Aim: so "all learners are able to access and participate in meaningful, challenging learning opportunities."
- Course use: design options in from the start (offer variations and props as defaults) rather than retrofitting for one student. UDL is a design framework, not an outcome trial.

## 9. Yoga Alliance

### 9a. RYS 200 standards (Elevated Standards, effective 2020)
Source: Yoga Alliance, "Standards for Registered Yoga Schools" PDF, https://yogaalliance.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/Standards-for-RYS-Credentials_NB22my-.pdf (file dated October 2025; read 2026-10-12). Tier: read-at-source.
- Effective date: "New standards underlying the RYS 200 credential took effect for newly registered schools starting February 27, 2020."
- Categories went from five to four: Techniques, Training and Practice; Anatomy and Physiology; Yoga Humanities; Professional Essentials (Teaching Methodology plus Practicum merged).
- Hours per category as printed on the RYS 200 pages: Techniques, Training, Practice 75 hours; Anatomy and Physiology 30 hours; Yoga Humanities 30 hours; Professional Essentials 50 hours (sum 185 of 200; the PDF pages read did not label the remaining 15).
- 12 competencies with sub-competencies; minimum 0.5 hours per sub-competency.
- Lead trainer must teach 150 of 200 hours; 1 to 5 lead trainers, all E-RYT 500.
- Distance learning allowed; minimum 15% of hours synchronous (RYS 200: 30 hours).
- Competency gate: "RYS programs should not graduate students simply because they have completed the teacher training."
- Professional Essentials includes Sequencing, Pace, Environment, Cueing, Class management. Asana competency includes "Complete sequencing (asana, pranayama, meditation) to achieve particular effect safely". Note: designing a multi-week PROGRAMME is not named as a competency on these pages.

### 9b. Scope of Practice
Source: Yoga Alliance Scope of Practice, https://yogaalliance.org/?p=2766, last updated February 27, 2020 (read 2026-10-12). Tier: read-at-source.
- The page does NOT use the words "diagnose", "treat" or "prescribe". It is framed as advice and credentials:
- "Members must not provide advice or services for which they are not properly and currently licensed".
- Unless competent and credentialed in the field, members must gain credentials, refer the student to a qualified professional, or decline.
- Members may provide referral lists of local resources, including medical and mental health professionals.
- Touch: adjustments only with "Explicit and Informed Consent"; "Previous consent does not imply future permission"; "Silence or lack of resistance, in and of itself, does not demonstrate consent."

Diagnose/treat wording lives in the separate Yoga Therapy Policy: https://yogaalliance.org/?p=3222, last updated November 18, 2021. Tier: read-at-source.
- Yoga Alliance does "not credential yoga therapy techniques, including the diagnosis or treatment of a mental or physical injury or illness."
- Members may not rely solely on RYS, RYT or YACEP designations to present themselves as qualified for yoga therapy.

## 10. Adherence and the habit myth

### 10a. Cramer H et al 2016 - dropout rates in yoga RCTs
Citation: Cramer H, Haller H, Dobos G, Lauche R. A systematic review and meta-analysis estimating the expected dropout rates in randomized controlled trials on yoga interventions. Evid Based Complement Alternat Med. 2016;2016:5859729. doi:10.1155/2016/5859729. PMID 27413387. PMCID PMC4927989.
Tier: read-at-source (abstract).
- 168 RCTs. "Overall dropout rate was 11.42% (95% confidence interval [CI] = 10.11%, 12.73%) in the yoga groups".
- Exercise controls slightly higher: 14.53%.
- Longer programmes: above 12 weeks, yoga dropout rose to 15.23%.
- Usually below 15-20%, but beyond 40% possible in some populations (HIV, heterogeneous age groups).
- Caveat: dropout from the trial is not the same as class attendance; Saper (1c) had a median of 7 of 12 classes.

### 10b. Attendance and home practice figures from the trials (see section 1, read-at-source)
- Sherman 2011: 65% of yoga participants attended at least 8 of 12 classes; median weekly home practice fell from 100 min (week 6) to 60 min (week 12).
- Tilbrook 2011: 60% attended at least 3 of the first 6 sessions and at least 3 others.
- Saper 2017: median 7 of 12 classes; 44% met the 9-class adherence bar; home practice median 27 min, 4 days a week, among the 75% who reported any.

### 10c. Reporting gap on progression and adherence in mind-body trials
Citation: Zhang W, Roster K, Hays RD, Wang C. Analysis of movement-based mind-body interventions to guide the implementation of osteoarthritis exercise programs: a descriptive review of randomized controlled trials. J Altern Complement Med. 2021;27(5):442-457. doi:10.1089/acm.2020.0420. PMID 33902322. PMCID PMC8881950.
Tier: read-at-source (abstract).
- 22 tai chi, qigong and yoga interventions (27 publications) scored on the 19-item CERT checklist.
- "starting level rule" (n = 1, 5%) and "progression rule" (n = 1, 5%) were the least reported items; planned fidelity/adherence 41%.
- Course point: most published programmes do not say how they progress, so a course on programme design is filling a real gap.

### 10d. Older adults - typical effective dose
Citation: Denham-Jones L, Gaskell L, Spence N, Pigott T. A systematic review of the effectiveness of yoga on pain, physical function, and quality of life in older adults with chronic musculoskeletal conditions. Musculoskeletal Care. 2022;20(1):47-73. doi:10.1002/msc.1576. PMID 34125986.
Tier: read-at-source (abstract).
- 11 studies, n = 2221. Effective trials used "at minimum one 60-min group class, and an average of four 30-min home practice sessions weekly."
- Supports props and modifications; "good adherence".

### 10e. "21 days to form a habit" vs Lally et al
Citation: Lally P, van Jaarsveld CHM, Potts HWW, Wardle J. How are habits formed: modelling habit formation in the real world. Eur J Soc Psychol. 2010;40(6):998-1009 (online 2009). doi:10.1002/ejsp.674.
Tier: read-at-source for the abstract lines; "reported" for the 66-day median.
- Design: 96 volunteers, one eating, drinking or activity behaviour, daily in the same context, 12 weeks (84 days); 82 analysed; curve fitted for 62, good fit for 39.
- Range (abstract): time to reach 95% of the automaticity asymptote "ranged from 18 to 254 days".
- Missing once: "Missing one opportunity to perform the behaviour did not materially affect the habit formation process."
- Median 66 days: NOT in the abstract; full text not reachable in this pass (Wiley PDF blocked). Widely reported as the median among the well-fitting participants. Tier: reported. Note also that estimates beyond day 84 are model extrapolations.
- "21 days": commonly traced to Maxwell Maltz, Psycho-Cybernetics (1960), about self-image adjustment, not habit formation. Tier: reported (book not read). No peer-reviewed source for "21 days" was found.
- "Beginners need 8 weeks": no primary source was found making this claim. The trials in section 1 used 12 weeks as a research convention, not a tested minimum.

## 11. When to refer (NHS)

### 11a. NHS back pain page
Source: NHS, "Back pain", https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/back-pain/, last reviewed 05 March 2026, next review 05 March 2029 (read 2026-10-12). Tier: read-at-source.
- See a GP if back pain: does not improve after a few weeks of home treatment; stops day-to-day activities; "you've lost weight without trying to"; lump, swelling or change in back shape; "does not improve after resting or is worse at night"; worse when sneezing, coughing or pooing; comes from the top of the back (between the shoulders).
- Urgent GP or NHS 111 if: "you feel hot, cold, shivery or generally unwell"; or "it's severe pain that starts suddenly, or it's getting worse quickly".
- Call 999 or go to A&E if: "pain, tingling, weakness or numbness in both legs"; "a loss of feeling around your genitals or anus"; changes in bladder or bowels; changes in sexual sensation or function; "chest pain"; it started after a serious accident.
- Course use: these are the stop-and-refer triggers a teacher can screen for verbally; the teacher refers, never diagnoses (see 9b).

### 11b. NHS yoga page
- https://www.nhs.uk/live-well/exercise/guide-to-yoga/ now resolves to a general exercise page with no yoga safety advice; https://www.nhs.uk/live-well/exercise/pilates-and-yoga/ is a video index with no safety text and no review date (both read 2026-10-12).
- Tier: could not verify. The NHS yoga safety advice often quoted (check with a GP if you have a health condition, tell the teacher, start with a beginner class) was NOT found on current nhs.uk pages. Do not cite it as NHS guidance unless an archived copy is located.
